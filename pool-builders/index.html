Fewer pools, same ad bill.

New pool construction has shrunk. POOLCORP, the biggest pool supply distributor in the US, estimates that just under 60,000 new pools were built in 2025, about half the pandemic peak and 40% below 2022, and expects about the same in 2026. In Texas, new pool permits fell about 9% in 2025, the third yearly drop in a row, with Dallas and Houston holding up better than the rest.

With fewer buyers, the builders who have a good year are rarely the ones who spend the most on ads. They’re the ones who turn more of the clicks they already pay for into design visits, and more design visits into signed pools.

Where design visits leak.

Between the ad and the design visit there are five places to lose a homeowner. Most builders have the ad and a page. Very few have the other three.

  • The ad makes no single promise. “Custom pools, financing, 20 years of experience, call today” gives nobody a reason to click now.
  • The page forgets the ad. The ad offers a free design visit and the page opens on “Custom pools built to last”, with no mention of it.
  • The proof is hidden. A 4.9 rating from 200 reviews sits on Google while the page the ad opens shows none of it.
  • Nothing for people who aren’t ready. Most homeowners clicking in October are planning for next summer. With nothing to take today, they leave.
  • The reply is slow, then stops. A form sent Tuesday evening gets a call back Thursday. One voicemail, then nothing.

A page that keeps the ad’s promise.

Every ad should open a page built for it, not your homepage.

  1. Repeat the promise in the headline

    If the ad says “Free backyard design visit”, the page’s first line says the same. People who clicked for that promise need to see it in the first second, or they assume they’re in the wrong place.

  2. Put your rating on the first screen

    Your Google rating and review count next to the button, plus one short review that mentions the build going smoothly. Homeowners are about to let a crew dig up their backyard; trust comes before the form.

  3. Give a realistic price range

    “Most of our pools land between $70,000 and $95,000” filters out people who can’t afford one and reassures those who can. You don’t lose anyone worth keeping by saying it.

  4. Make booking one tap away

    A short form (name, phone, zip code) or two design visit times, visible without scrolling on a phone. Every extra field and every scroll loses people.

  5. Show your pools, not stock photos

    Three or four of your own builds in the same area, with the city named. Homeowners want to see pools that could be in their neighborhood.

A free offer for homeowners not ready yet.

A homeowner who saw your ad in the fall may not sign until spring. If the only option is “request a quote”, most of them leave. Give them something worth asking for now:

  • A free backyard design visit, with a 3D drawing of their pool.
  • A price guide for their area: what a pool, a spa and decking typically cost, and what changes the number.
  • A spring build slot list: “Get on the list for an April or May build.”

Each of these gets you a name and number months before they choose a builder, and the follow-up does the rest.

What a good first reply looks like.

Speed matters more than it feels like it should. In a study of 1.25 million online leads at 42 US companies, firms that responded within an hour were about seven times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that responded even one hour later. Here is a reply that answers the question and moves straight to a design visit:

Hi Dana, thanks for reaching out. Most of our pools with a spa land between $75,000 and $95,000, depending on size, decking and features. The exact number comes from a free design visit at your home.

I have Thursday at 5pm or Saturday at 10am. Which works?

And here is the reply many builders send the next day:

Thanks for your interest! Please give us a call to discuss your project.

The second one answers nothing and asks a busy homeowner to make a phone call during the same hours they couldn’t call the first time.

Follow-up until they decide.

Homeowners go quiet because they’re busy, not because they said no. Short, personal follow-ups by text and email keep you in the running: day 1 (photos of a pool you built nearby), day 3 (answers to the question most homeowners ask next, usually financing or timeline), day 7 (open design visit times this week) and day 14 (a friendly last check-in). Stop the moment they reply.

After the design visit, send the proposal the same day and check in two days later. Many builders lose signed pools here, not at the first reply.

Test your own path in five minutes.

  1. Find your live ads in the Meta Ad Library and the Google Ads Transparency Center, and write down what each one promises.
  2. Tap through each ad on your phone. Does the page repeat the promise? Is your rating on the first screen? How many scrolls to the form?
  3. On a Tuesday evening, send a quote request through your own form from a personal email.
  4. Write down when the first real reply arrives, and whether it gave a price range and two times.

Any “no”, or a reply that took more than an hour, is where design visits are going. That’s exactly what our free leak check covers, on video.
